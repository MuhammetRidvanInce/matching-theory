## Robustness Check: Correlated Student Preferences

To examine whether the welfare comparison between GSSOSM and MCSDM depends on the assumed structure of student preferences, an additional robustness analysis was conducted for the market with 100 students and 10 colleges. In the baseline model, students' rankings over colleges are generated independently, producing heterogeneous preference profiles. In the alternative scenario, each college is assigned a common quality component that enters every student's latent utility, together with an equally weighted student-specific random component. This specification creates positively correlated student preferences while preserving individual heterogeneity. The two mechanisms were evaluated using the same 100 seed values, while the numbers of students and colleges, college types, quotas, score distributions, and utility-index calculation were kept unchanged. The results are reported in Table 7.

**Table 7. Robustness Check under Independent and Correlated Student Preferences**

| Student preference structure | MCSDM mean UI (SD) | GSSOSM mean UI (SD) | Mean difference | GSSOSM higher / equal / lower | Paired t-statistic | Paired t-test p-value | Wilcoxon p-value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Baseline: independent heterogeneous preferences | 0.8220 (0.0500) | 0.8360 (0.0520) | +0.0140 (+1.68%) | 30 / 70 / 0 | 8.770 | <0.001 | <0.001 |
| Alternative: correlated preferences | 0.4987 (0.0728) | 0.4993 (0.0728) | +0.0006 (+0.12%) | 11 / 89 / 0 | 3.032 | 0.003 | <0.001 |

*Notes:* UI denotes the student utility index and SD denotes the sample standard deviation across 100 simulations. The percentage difference is calculated relative to the MCSDM mean. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test is calculated over non-zero paired differences. Student preferences in the correlated scenario are generated from an equally weighted common college-quality component and a student-specific random component.

Table 7 shows that the qualitative welfare ordering of the mechanisms is unchanged when student preferences become positively correlated. Under the baseline independent-preference structure, GSSOSM produces a higher utility index in 30 simulations and the same utility index in the remaining 70 simulations. Under correlated preferences, the average utility levels of both mechanisms decline substantially, which is consistent with stronger competition for commonly preferred colleges. Nevertheless, GSSOSM remains weakly superior: it produces a higher student utility index in 11 simulations, an equal index in 89 simulations, and a lower index in none. Although the average gain falls to approximately 0.0006 utility-index points, or 0.12% relative to MCSDM, the paired difference remains statistically significant according to both the paired t-test (t = 3.032, p = 0.003) and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (p < 0.001). These findings indicate that the main conclusion—GSSOSM yields student welfare outcomes that are at least as high as those of MCSDM—is robust to a preference environment in which students tend to rank the same colleges highly.
